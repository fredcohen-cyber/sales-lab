# Sales Playbook

Oct 10, 2026 · Fred Cohen

Winning reps lead the sale, ask more than they tell, and always ask for a decision. This playbook shows how to do that at every stage, from the opener to the follow-up.

Each section gives the principle and the exact words to use, followed by **Coaching notes** on applying them.

## The playbook on one page

Ten rules carry most of the playbook; the call flow below shows where each section fits.

1. Your job is to help the client decide. Leading is not being pushy.
2. Ask for 30 seconds instead of assuming you have them.
3. The prospect talks 80% of the time, and your 20% is mostly questions.
4. Find the gap: what the problem costs them in dollars or hours each week.
5. Recommend one option with a clear plan: problem, solution, game plan.
6. Sell what it unlocks, not what it costs.
7. Ask for the business directly, then stop talking.
8. Treat every objection as a request for clarity, and isolate it before you answer it.
9. Never “just check in.” Every touch carries an idea, a decision, or a next step.
10. End every call with a yes, a no, or a dated next step.

An objection sends you back to the close, not to the start. A prospect who goes quiet gets follow-ups with a reason, then a takeaway message, then no more chasing.

## 1. Mindset: lead the sale

Prospects stall because deciding is uncomfortable, so your job is to make the decision easy, not to wait for it.

- **You're the expert, so lead.** The passive rep says “take your time, I'll email you some details” and never hears back. A leader directs the conversation and helps the client reach a decision.
- **Give them a plan.** People say yes to someone who has already planned the evening: where, when, what to wear. Prospects respond the same way to “here's the problem, the solution, and the plan.”

### The closer's six disciplines

| Discipline | On the call |
| --- | --- |
| Silence | Ask your question, then wait. Whoever fills the silence gives something up. |
| Frame | You're the doctor: diagnose before you prescribe. If they ask for the price first, steer back to discovery. |
| Disqualify | Say it out loud when someone may not be a fit; good-fit prospects then sell themselves. Stop rescheduling people who aren't serious. |
| Boundaries | Every call ends in a yes or a no. Stop chasing. |
| Detachment | Never let them feel you need this deal more than they do. |
| Directness | If something feels off, name it. |

### Coaching notes

- **Leading is not pressuring.** The test: are you pushing toward their goal or your quota? Leading means no call ends without clarity; it never means trapping anyone.
- **Pressure shows up later.** Deals pushed across the line tend to come back as cancellations, refund requests, and slow payments. Stay on the leadership side of that line.
- **Keep directness curious, not accusatory.** Try: *“I might be reading this wrong, but it feels like something's holding you back. What is it?”*
- **Detachment comes from a full pipeline.** It's hard to sound unattached with two live deals. Prospecting volume is a mindset tool as much as a numbers tool.
- **A frame-control line.** When they jump straight to price: *“Happy to get you a number. So it's the right number, can I ask you three quick questions first?”*
- **Allow one more outcome when a group decides.** With partners or a committee, a dated next step with the decision-makers on it is also a valid ending. “I'll follow up” never is.

## 2. Openers: earning the first 30 seconds

On a cold call, the only thing to win in the first ten seconds is permission to keep talking.

### The permission opener

*“I'll be upfront, this is a sales call. Do you want to hang up on me, or can I get 30 seconds to tell you why I'm calling?”*

People hang up on pitches, not on someone asking permission. Offering the choice flips their thinking from “how do I get off this call” to “sure, go ahead.”

### Value-first openers

Most trade 30 to 60 seconds for a business outcome.

- *“Give me 30 seconds and I'll show you how I can be an asset to your company.”*
- *“Give me 60 seconds and I'll show you how to generate more revenue.”*
- *“If I could raise your revenue without touching the price, is that worth a minute?”*
- *“Quick question: are you the one bringing in the revenue?”*
- *“If I could help you generate more revenue this month, is this call worth 60 seconds?”*

### The soft lead-in

*“I'm not sure if it's for you, but...”* and then the idea.

It takes the pressure off and invites the listener to be the judge, so they lean in. Use it for an offer, an invitation, or a next step.

### Coaching notes

- **Use the revenue-owner question with care.** “Are you the one bringing in the revenue?” can land like “Are you the decision-maker?”, which puts people on the defensive. Use it only when you truly don't know who you've reached.
- **Plan what comes after the 30 seconds.** Use four beats, about 20 seconds in all:
    1. Who you are, in one line.
    2. Why you're calling them: something specific to their business.
    3. The result you deliver, with one proof point.
    4. A question that opens a conversation: *“Is that something you're dealing with, or are you in good shape there?”*
- **Translate “revenue” into their unit.** Owners count in their own terms: signed cases for a law firm, booked patients for a medical practice. “More signed cases this month” lands harder than “more revenue,” as long as the proof is ready for the next sentence.
- **Clear the scripts with counsel.** Calling and texting are regulated (TCPA, do-not-call lists, state rules). Get sign-off on the dialing setup and any text outreach before scaling.

## 3. Discovery: let them sell themselves

The rep who asks better questions beats the rep with better answers, so the prospect should do about 80% of the talking.

- **80/20.** They talk 80% of the time. Your 20% is mostly questions.
- **Ask, then go silent.** Don't explain or soften the question.
- **Stop when you get a yes.** Every extra detail is one more thing to disagree with.

### The discovery sequence

Work through these in order:

| Step | Ask | What it gets you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Destination | “What are you hoping to accomplish?” or “Where do you want to be 12 months from now?” | Their goal, in their words |
| 2. Distance | “How far are you from that right now?” | Room for your solution |
| 3. Gap | “What's not working, and how long has it been going on?” | The problem, and how long they've lived with it |
| 4. Cost | “What is that costing you each week, in dollars or hours?” | The value of solving it |
| 5. Consequence | “What happens if nothing changes?” | The price of doing nothing |
| 6. Vision | “If we solved this, would that help? What would things look like once it's gone?” | Their own link between your solution and their problem |

### When they're “just looking”

Inbound leads often open with “we're just exploring options.” Don't hover with a pushy demo, and don't vanish behind a brochure; offer direction.

- *“Is there anything in particular you're looking for? Maybe I can save you some time by pointing you in the right direction.”*
- *“Totally fair, most people are when they first reach out. What prompted you to start looking now?”*

### Coaching notes

- **Do the math out loud, with their numbers.** “Ten hours a week is about 500 hours a year. What's an hour of your time worth?” Your estimate is a pitch; their number is a fact, and it's what makes your price feel like a discount.
- **Write down their exact words.** The close in Section 5 plays their problem back in their language. People believe what they say themselves; Pascal made the same point in the 1600s.
- **Add three qualifying questions: budget, decision-makers, timing.** Skip them here and they surface at the close as “can't afford it,” “need to talk to my partner,” and “not right now.” For the people question, ask: *“Who else should be part of this decision?”*
- **Ask fewer questions and dig deeper.** Asked like a checklist, these questions feel like a trap. Ask one, then follow it: “Tell me more.” “What has that cost you?” “What have you tried?”
- **Go beyond the numbers.** Ask what success would look like for them personally: *“What would this year look like if it went right?”*

## 4. Presenting: one recommendation, one plan

Present one solution, with certainty, as a simple plan built from what they told you in discovery.

- **One option, not three.** Basic, standard, and premium change the question from whether to buy to which to buy, and people who can't pick one pick none. Buyers don't want options; they want someone confident enough to tell them what they need.
- **Lay out A = B = C.** “Here's your problem, here's the solution, here's the game plan.” Without that structure, prospects overload and say “just send it over.”
- **Sell what it unlocks.** Lead with the outcome, the transformation, and the support behind it, not the price.
- **Explain the impact before the number.** Then state the number without rushing to justify it.

### The recommendation template

*“Based on what you told me, [their problem, in their words], here's what I recommend: [one solution]. Here's the plan: [step 1], [step 2], [step 3]. The investment is [price]. Against the [monthly cost] you said this is costing you, it pays for itself in [time].”*

Then stop talking.

### Coaching notes

- **One option is earned in discovery.** A single recommendation sounds confident when it's clearly built from what they said. Without that, it sounds like one-size-fits-all.
- **Save two choices for logistics.** “Does Tuesday or Thursday work?” keeps things moving; offering two versions of the product stalls them.
- **Tiered pricing? Still lead with one.** Recommend the tier that fits. Mention the others only if they ask or the budget truly requires it.
- **This matters most in complex sales.** Too many choices stall buyers most when options are hard to compare and they're unsure what they need, which describes most B2B service decisions.
- **Never ask “Can you afford it?”** Ask whether it fits their plans: *“Does this fit the growth you're planning for this year?”*

## 5. Closing: ask for the business

Once pricing is on the table, ask a direct question that calls for a decision, then stop talking.

### Retire these

“So what do you think?” “What would you like to do from here?” “What would you like to do as next steps?” Each one hands the momentum back to the prospect.

### Use these instead

- *“Have you heard and seen enough to make a decision?”*
- *“If I put that in writing, do we have a deal?”*
- *“Are you ready to get started?”*

### The sequence

1. **Recap in their words:** *“Let me make sure I have this right. You're dealing with [X], you want [Y], and this does [Z]. Is there anything we're missing?”*
2. **Ask directly.**
3. **Go silent.**
4. **If yes, stop selling** and move to paperwork, start date, and kickoff; more talk reopens the decision. **If not, go to Section 6.**

### Coaching notes

- **Know when an open question can close.** After the prospect has described the goal, the cost, and life after the fix, *“What's the next step you'd like to take?”* hands them the pen. After a pitch, it hands them the exit, so default to the direct close.
- **A “no” to “Have you heard enough?” is useful.** It surfaces the real objection. Follow with: *“What else do you need to see?”*
- **With several decision-makers, close for the meeting.** On an early call, the close is a dated meeting with everyone who decides. “Who else should join?” gets them in the room.

## 6. Objections: each one is a request for clarity

The first objection decides the sale: treat it as a request for clarity, not an exit, and find the real issue before answering it.

### The pattern

1. **Acknowledge.** “Of course.” “I completely respect that.” Not “no problem.”
2. **Isolate.** Find the real issue: price, value, timing, information, or another person.
3. **Answer it** with the play below.
4. **Ask again** with a direct close.

### “I need to think about it” or “I need to sleep on it”

- *“What specifically do you need to think through?”*
- *“So I can understand, is it the price, the timing, or more information you need?”*
- *“Of course. Is there anything about the numbers that doesn't sit right?”*
- *“I completely respect that. Let me make sure I understand: you have [X], you want [Y], and this does [Z]. Is there something we're missing?”*

Never “No problem, take your time.” That lets the deal drift.

### “It's too expensive”

*“I understand. When you say too expensive, is it outside your budget, or are you not seeing enough value for the price?”*

A value objection is a request to justify the price in business terms. Go back to the cost number from discovery and show the return; never offer a discount.

### “I can't afford it right now”

*“That's fair. If the numbers made sense for you today, would you feel comfortable moving forward today?”*

### “Your competitor charges less”

Don't defend your price. Let them explain it:

*“Even though we charge more than our competitors, our clients keep coming back year after year. Why do you think that is?”*

They'll say better service or better results. Then: *“Exactly. If you want this done as cheaply as possible, I'll be upfront, we're not the right fit. If you want the best result, it makes sense to keep talking. What would you like to do?”*

### “Will you lower your price?”

Agree with their goal first, then reframe the risk:

*“Great question. I'm guessing the thinking is that the less you pay, the more you keep, and that's my goal for you too. Would you fly an airline whose slogan was ‘our pilots work for the lowest wages’? Trusting [what's at stake] to whoever charges least is the same bet. That doesn't make sense, does it?”*

### “I'm already working with someone”

Compliment, get curious, create contrast:

1. *“Thanks for sharing that. For a decision this important, I respect that you've already sought advice. I do the same.”*
2. *“Can I ask what matters most to you as you compare the advice you're getting?”*
3. *“Do you feel completely confident you're getting everything you need, or are you open to a second opinion?”*

The short version: *“That's actually good. What made you pick up my call if you already have someone?”*

Never criticize the competitor. Make the buyer feel smart instead.

### “Just send me some information”

Agreeing (“sure, I'll email you some details”) is the passive move, and that email rarely gets a reply. Lead instead:

- *“Happy to. So I send the right thing, can I ask two quick questions?”*
- *“Does it make sense to take 15 minutes Thursday to walk through it together?”*

### “I need to talk to my partner”

- *“Makes sense. What do you think they'll want to know?”*
- *“Does it make sense to set 20 minutes with both of you, so they hear it firsthand?”*

### Coaching notes

- **“Think about it” is rarely the real objection.** It usually covers price, risk, or a missing decision-maker. If they truly need time, leave with a date, a time, and who'll be on the call.
- **Split budget from value.** A value problem means the cost math in discovery wasn't strong enough, so go back to it. A budget problem means qualification was skipped.
- **Never give a naked discount.** If you move, trade something: a longer term, payment upfront, a smaller scope, a later start. A cut with nothing in return tells them the first price was padded, so “if the numbers made sense” should mean structure, not a lower price.
- **The competitor play only works if it's true.** Have real retention figures and client results ready for the moment they answer. Its closing question works because they just named your value themselves (see Section 5).
- **Fit the analogy to the buyer.** The pilot line works because the stakes are obvious. Pick a comparison from your buyer's world where cheapest is clearly risky; for a law firm: *“Would you tell a client to hire the cheapest expert witness?”*
- **Soften “What made you pick up?”** Said flatly, it sounds like a gotcha; with a smile, it gets them to say what's missing. Then ask when the current arrangement next comes up for review, which gives you a dated reason to call back.

## 7. Follow-up and re-engagement

Every follow-up needs a reason to respond, whether an idea, a decision, or a next step; never “just checking in.”

“Just following up,” “just checking in,” and “any questions?” signal neediness and give the prospect nothing to answer. They also set the terms of the relationship: you'll keep chasing until they feel like replying.

### What to say instead

- **Bring something:** *“Quick idea for you...”*
- **Ask about the next step:** *“Calling to see if it makes sense to [get your team started this month].”*
- **Offer two concrete options:** *“Does Tuesday or Thursday work?”*
- **Lower the pressure:** *“I'm not sure if it's for you, but...”*

### When they go silent: the takeaway message

Stop chasing. Remove your need for the sale and hand them the decision:

*“Hi [Name], this is [you]. I've reached out a couple of times and haven't heard back, so I'll assume you've gone in another direction, which is completely okay. I wish you the best with [their project]. If I'm mistaken and you'd still like to talk, I'll be glad to help.”*

It lowers the pressure and often draws a reply out of respect.

### Coaching notes

**Run a set cadence, then stop.** A suggested sequence after a meeting that ended without a decision; stretch it for longer sales cycles.

| When | Touch | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day 1 | Recap email: their problem, your recommendation, their numbers | Put the decision in writing |
| Days 4–5 | “Quick idea” with something new, such as a result or case study | Add value |
| Days 8–10 | “Does it make sense to...” call | Ask for the next step |
| Days 14–21 | Takeaway message | Close the loop |

- **Honor the takeaway.** A “just checking in” a week later destroys its credibility. If they reply, restart where the deal stalled, not from scratch.
- **Keep it generous.** The message works by releasing pressure. If your version reads as a guilt trip, rewrite it.
- **Record why deals go quiet.** Logging the real reason in the CRM shows which objection discovery keeps missing.

## 8. Language guide: say this, not that

Small word choices signal either authority or neediness. These swaps are listed in the order they come up in a sale.

| Instead of | Say | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Sorry to bother you” | “I'll keep this brief” | Frames you as an annoyance |
| “To be honest...” | “Here's the trade-off” or “I'll be upfront” | Implies you weren't honest before |
| “Are you the decision-maker?” | “Who else should join?” | Attacks their status |
| “Trust me” | “Here's proof” or “Let me show you the results” | Implies they shouldn't |
| “Let's try this” | “Let's explore this” | Implies it might fail |
| “This costs [price]” | “Here's what this unlocks” | Leads with price, not outcome |
| “Expensive” | “Investment” | Frames cost instead of value |
| “We can discount it” | “Here's your ROI” | Says the first price was fake |
| “Unfortunately” | “Let's do this instead” | Leads with the negative |
| “Let me know if you have questions” | “What questions do you have?” | Invites silence |
| “What do you think?” | “Have you heard enough to make a decision?” | Hands back the momentum |
| “Let me know if you're interested” | “Here's the next step” or “Does A or B work?” | Puts the work on them |
| “No problem, take your time” | “What specifically do you need to think through?” | Lets the deal drift |
| “No problem” | “Absolutely” or “Of course” | Hints there could have been a problem |
| “Just checking in” or “I wanted to follow up” | “Quick idea for you” or “Does it make sense to [next step]?” | Signals need and sets up a chase |

### Coaching notes

- **Wording supports substance; it doesn't replace it.** A prospect forgives “just checking in” from a rep who clearly understands their business. Perfect wording won't save a rep who doesn't.
- **Don't overcorrect.** “Investment” in every sentence sounds scripted. Use the swaps where they fit naturally.

## 9. Practice and coaching

Scripts work only once they sound natural, and that takes repetition: the best reps run their pitch hundreds of times.

### Weekly drills

- **Objection draw.** Put the Section 6 objections on cards. Each rep draws one and answers in 30 seconds, and the team checks for acknowledge, isolate, answer, ask again.
- **Opener round.** Each rep delivers an opener plus the four-beat follow-through from Section 2. Keep the openers that earn a “sure, go ahead.”
- **Discovery role-play.** One rep plays a prospect with a hidden cost. The other has to surface it as a weekly dollar or hour figure.
- **Closing reps.** Ask “Have you heard enough to make a decision?” and hold the silence until the other person speaks.

### Call review checklist

Score one recorded call per rep per week.

1. Did the opener ask for permission or time?
2. Did the prospect talk more than the rep?
3. Did discovery produce a cost in dollars or hours?
4. Was one option recommended, with a clear plan?
5. Did the rep ask for the business directly?
6. Was the first objection isolated before it was answered?
7. Did the call end in a yes, a no, or a dated next step?
8. Did any phrase from the left column of Section 8 slip in?

### What to track

- Conversations started per opener, to keep the openers that work.
- Win rate after the first objection, to see whether the Section 6 plays hold up.
- Share of calls that end without a dated next step.
- Reply rate to the takeaway message.
